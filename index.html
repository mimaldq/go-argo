The World Trade Organization (WTO)

The WTO is the only global international organization dealing with the rules of trade between nations. At its heart are the WTO agreements, negotiated and signed by the bulk of the world's trading nations and ratified in their parliaments.

The goal is to ensure that trade flows as smoothly, predictably and freely as possible, helping to raise standards of living, create jobs, and promote sustainable development worldwide.

About the World Trade Organization

The World Trade Organization (WTO) is the only global international organization that deals with the rules of trade between nations. It was established on January 1, 1995, replacing the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) that had been in operation since 1948.

The WTO's primary purpose is to open trade for the benefit of all. Its main functions include:

Administering Trade Agreements

The WTO administers the multilateral trade agreements that form the legal ground rules for international commerce.

Forum for Trade Negotiations

It serves as a forum for negotiating agreements aimed at reducing obstacles to international trade.

Dispute Settlement

The WTO provides a mechanism for resolving trade disputes between member countries.

The WTO is run by its member governments. All major decisions are made by the membership as a whole, either by ministers (who meet at least once every two years) or by their ambassadors or delegates (who meet regularly in Geneva).

Key Functions of the WTO

The WTO performs several critical functions in the global trading system:

Implementing Agreements

The WTO oversees the implementation, administration, and operation of the covered agreements, providing the common institutional framework for the conduct of trade relations among its members.

Negotiation Forum

It provides a forum for negotiations among its members concerning their multilateral trade relations in matters dealt with under the agreements.

Dispute Settlement

The WTO administers the integrated dispute settlement system, which is a central element in providing security and predictability to the multilateral trading system.

Trade Policy Review

The WTO conducts regular reviews of individual members' trade policies and practices through the Trade Policy Review Mechanism (TPRM).

Technical Assistance

The WTO provides technical assistance and training for developing countries to help them build their trade capacity and participate more fully in the global trading system.

Monitoring

The WTO monitors national trade policies and identifies trade issues that need attention, helping to ensure transparency and predictability in international trade.

History of the WTO

The WTO's creation in 1995 marked the biggest reform of international trade since the end of the Second World War. Its history can be traced through several key milestones:

1947

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) is signed by 23 countries as a provisional agreement to reduce trade barriers.

1948-1994

GATT becomes the de facto global trade organization, overseeing eight rounds of multilateral trade negotiations that progressively reduced tariffs.

1986-1994

The Uruguay Round of trade negotiations takes place, resulting in the most comprehensive set of trade agreements in history and the decision to create the WTO.

1995

The World Trade Organization officially begins operations on January 1, replacing the GATT system with a more powerful and comprehensive institution.

2001

The Doha Development Agenda is launched, focusing on addressing the needs of developing countries. China becomes a WTO member after 15 years of negotiations.

2013

The Bali Package is agreed upon, the first major agreement under the WTO, focusing on trade facilitation, agriculture, and development issues.

2015

The Nairobi Package is adopted, including decisions on agriculture, cotton, and issues related to least-developed countries.

2022

WTO members reach historic agreements on fisheries subsidies and extend the moratorium on e-commerce tariffs at the 12th Ministerial Conference.

Fundamental Principles of the WTO

The WTO trading system is founded on several fundamental principles that form the basis of the multilateral trading system:

Most-Favored-Nation (MFN)

Under the WTO agreements, countries cannot normally discriminate between their trading partners. If a country improves the benefits it gives to one trading partner, it must give the same treatment to all other WTO members.

National Treatment

Imported and locally-produced goods should be treated equally — at least after the foreign goods have entered the market. The same applies to foreign and domestic services, and to foreign and local trademarks, copyrights, and patents.

Freer Trade

Lowering trade barriers is one of the most obvious means of encouraging trade. These barriers include customs duties (or tariffs) and measures such as import bans or quotas that restrict quantities selectively.

Predictability and Transparency

With stability and predictability, investment is encouraged, jobs are created, and consumers can fully enjoy the benefits of competition — choice and lower prices.

Promoting Fair Competition

The WTO is not a "free trade" institution as it allows tariffs and, in limited circumstances, other forms of protection. It is a system of rules dedicated to open, fair, and undistorted competition.

Development and Economic Reform

The WTO agreements contain special provisions for developing countries, including longer periods to implement agreements and commitments, measures to increase trading opportunities, and support to build trade capacity.

These principles are the foundation of the multilateral trading system and are designed to ensure that trade flows as smoothly, predictably, and freely as possible.
